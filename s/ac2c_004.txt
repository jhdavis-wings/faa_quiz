 CHAPTER 3. COMMUNICATION, NAVIGATION, AND EMERGENCY LOCATOR
   TRANSMITTER SYSTEM INSTALLATIONS

 3.1 Purpose. This chapter describes installation considerations and requirements for basic
   standalone, installations of communication, navigation, and emergency locator
   transmitter (ELT) equipment.

   Note: Standalone installations do not depend on other systems or complex
   interfaces to function.

 3.2 Hazards and Warnings.

3.2.1 When installing these systems, follow the aircraft and equipment manufacturers’
   instructions, as appropriate. Practice a “clean as you go” philosophy, which entails
   maintaining cleanliness and order. Ensure that equipment and systems function properly
   and perform their intended function(s).

3.2.2 Alterations of aircraft that are performed to accommodate the installation of radio
   equipment must be evaluated for their impact on aircraft design and operation. Refer to
   AC 23.1309-1, System Safety Analysis and Assessment for Part 23 Airplanes, for
   additional information concerning the evaluation for equipment, systems, and
   installations in 14 CFR part 23 airplanes.

3.2.3 Frequently, an alteration to accommodate the installation of radio equipment will have
   little impact on the design or operation of an aircraft; however, all potential elements of
   impact must be considered. One approach is to evaluate each element independently.

3.2.4 Consider the impact when Weight and Balance (W&B) or structural load limits of an
   added system exceeds existing installations. Consider the impact when radio frequency,
   such as electromagnetic interference (EMI), High Intensity Radiated Fields (HIRF), or
   lightning, may negatively affect existing systems (e.g., accuracy of the magnetic
   compass).

3.2.5 When evaluating elements of impact, consider the before and after states. No further
   analysis of W&B may be required if an object of similar size and weight was previously
   installed in a location. If mounting attach points were previously substantiated to support
   a specific load and the same load or less is being installed, the previous analysis may be
   referenced.

   Note: Data that is referenced must be available and reviewed.

3.2.6 When structures must be fabricated or reinforced, the standard practices approved for
   repairs, if applicable, may be employed. AC 43.13-1, Acceptable Methods, Techniques,
   and Practices—Aircraft Inspection and Repair, may provide structural design data for
   fabrication of mountings and attachments.

3.2.7 Ensure that the capacity of the aircraft’s charging system is not exceeded, including any
   required additional allowances.

 3.2.8 Care should be taken to ensure that cables or wires will not interfere with the aircraft’s
   flight, engine, or propeller controls.

 3.2.9 When removing older radios, wiring, or power supplies and installing newer solid-state
   components, weigh the old equipment and perform a new W&B calculation. This is
   important since differences in the location and weight of equipment will shift the center
   of gravity (CG).

3.2.10 Refer to AC 43.13-1, Chapter 10, Weight and Balance, and FAA-H-8083-1, Aircraft

  3.3 Considerations When Installing Avionics Equipment. When installing radio
   equipment, use areas or locations designated by the airframe manufacturer and use
   factory-supplied brackets or racks. Follow the aircraft manufacturer’s installation
   instructions. When this information is not available, use locations in the aircraft of known
   load-carrying capabilities. Baggage compartments, cabins, or flight deck floors are good
   mounting platforms provided the floor attachments meet the strength requirements.
   Another method is to fabricate support racks, brackets, or shelves and attach them to the
   aircraft structure to provide a mounting that will withstand the inertia forces stipulated in
   Chapter 2, Structural Data.

 3.3.1 General Considerations. Ensure the following:

   3.3.1.1  There is appropriate air circulation to ensure proper cooling and dissipation of
   any heat generated or present. Consider the flammability characteristics of all
   associated elements.

   3.3.1.2  There are appropriate clearances to prevent mechanical damage to other parts
   of the aircraft or from other parts of the aircraft.

   3.3.1.3  There is protection provided to the component or article from any fluids or
   fumes that may be expected and that the component or article will not cause
   any fluids or fumes to be present that may result in damage to the aircraft or
   its occupants.

   3.3.1.4  That any interference, environmental or operational, to an aircraft or any
   system of the aircraft is identified and minimized as to not affect airworthiness
   of the aircraft.

   3.3.1.5  That flight characteristics of the aircraft are not altered unless appropriately
   identified and the changes are within the certified design limits.

 3.4 Instrument Panel Mounting. This paragraph is supplemented by AC 43.13-1,
   Chapter 4, Metal Structure, Welding, and Brazing, and is applicable to the installation of
   radio units in instrument panels.

3.4.1 Stationary Instrument Panels—Nonstructural and Structural. The stationary instrument
   panel in some aircraft is part of the primary structure. Prior to making any additional
   cutouts or enlargements of an existing cutout, determine if the panel is part of the primary
   structure. If the panel is structural, make additional cutouts or the enlargement of existing
   cutouts in accordance with the aircraft manufacturer’s instructions, or substantiate the
   structural integrity of the altered panel in a manner acceptable to the Administrator.
   Radius all corners and remove all burrs from the cutout and drilled holes.

3.4.2 Added Equipment Stationary Instrument Panel. When radio equipment is to be installed
   in a stationary panel already supporting instruments, glove compartments, etc., determine
   the capability of the panel to support the total load.

3.4.3 Case Support. To minimize the load on a stationary instrument panel, whenever
   practicable, install a support between the rear (or side) surface of the radio case and a
   nearby structural member of the aircraft (see Figure 3-2, Typical Fabricated Panel
   Mounting).

3.4.4 Added Equipment—Shock-Mounted Panels. When installing radio equipment designed
   for use in shock-mounted panels, the total accumulated weight of equipment installed
   must not exceed the weight carrying capabilities of the shock mounts. Determine whether
   the structure to which the shock mounts are connected is satisfactory for supporting the
   added weight.

3.4.5 Existing Factory Fasteners. When possible, use existing plate nuts and machine screws
   provided by the aircraft manufacturer to attach the radio case or rack. If additional
   fastening is required, use machine screws and elastic stop nuts (preferably plate nuts).

 3.5 Other Mounting Areas. The following are acceptable methods for installing radio
   equipment at other than instrument panel locations.

3.5.1 Shock-Mounted Units.

   3.5.1.1  Wood or Composition Flooring. Secure the shock-mounted base assembly
   (suitable for radio unit) directly to the floor using machine screws. Add a
   doubler to the bottom of the floor, thereby sandwiching the composition floor
   between each shock-mount foot and the doubler. Subsequent removal and
   reinstallation of the shock-mount foot will be facilitated if plate nuts are
   secured to the doubler. Where practicable, use small retaining screws to keep
   the doubler in position. Install a ground strap between the radio rack and metal
   structure of the aircraft.

 3.6 Fabrication of Supporting Brackets for Attachment to a Structure Other Than

   unit mounting base assembly can be installed in the same manner as described in

 3.8 Electrical Requirements.

3.8.1 Installing an electrical system or component into an aircraft requires consideration of the
   electrical load, the appropriate power distribution circuit, and available power capacity.
   The specific requirements for a system depend on its electrical characteristics and the
   criticality of its application for use in the aircraft’s operation.

3.8.2 The total energy available to power electrical systems is referred to as the aircraft’s
   capacity. It includes available stored power and generated power. This will vary
   depending on the phase of flight or type of operation.

3.8.3 The critical distribution circuit of an aircraft is designed to transfer power from the source
   to a system determined to be critical to the operation or function of the aircraft. This
   circuit is required to include additional capacity and circuit protection as required by
   applicable regulations. Distribution systems should be designed to facilitate load-
   shedding procedures. Power distribution system design includes the following concerns.
   Refer to AC 43.13-1, Chapter 11, Aircraft Electrical Systems, for:

   •  Circuit protection.
   •  Wire selection.
   •  Connectors, switches, and termination devices.
   •  Wire routing.
   •  Wiring/cable support.
   •  Identification.

3.8.4 Installation of Wiring. Select and use wiring of a type and design that is appropriate for
   the intended purpose of the application. Install in a manner suitably protected from fuel,
   oil, water, other detrimental substances, oxygen systems, and abrasion damages.

3.8.5 Power Sources.

   3.8.5.1  Connect radio electrical systems to the aircraft electrical system at the power
   source protective device, a terminal strip, or use a plug and receptacle
   connection.

   3.8.5.2  Radio electrical systems must function properly whether connected in
   combination or independent.

3.8.6 Protective Devices.

   3.8.6.1  Incorporate a “trip free” (one that will trip open if the ON-OFF mechanism is
   held in the ON position) resettable type circuit breaker or a fuse in the power
   supply from the bus. Mount in a manner accessible to a crewmember during
   flight for circuit breaker resetting or fuse replacement and label.

   3.8.6.2  Select circuit breakers or fuses that will provide adequate protection against
   overloading of the radio system wiring.

   3.8.6.3  Connect all leads in such a manner that the aircraft’s master switch will
   interrupt the circuit when the master switch is opened, unless the equipment is
   intended to be powered when the master switch is open.

   3.8.6.4  Radio system controls are to provide independent operation of each system
   installed and be clearly labeled to identify their function relative to the unit of
   equipment they operate.

3.8.7 Wire Bundle Separation From Flammable Fluid Lines.

   3.8.7.1  Physically separate radio electric wire bundles from lines or equipment
   containing oil, fuel, hydraulic fluid, alcohol, or oxygen.

   3.8.7.2  Mount radio electrical wire bundles above or on level with flammable fluid
   lines and whenever possible, maintain a minimum separation of 6 inches.
   Where separation is 1/2 to 2 inches, install clamps. Refer to AC 43.13-1,
   Chapter 11, Paragraph 11-127, Flammable Fluids and Gases.

3.8.8 Cable Attachment to Shock-Mounted Units. Route and support electrical wire bundles
   and mechanical cables in a manner that will allow normal motion of equipment without
   strain or damage to the wire bundles or mechanical cables.

3.8.9 Radio Bonding. It is advisable to electrically bond radio equipment to the aircraft in order
   to provide a low impedance ground and to minimize radio interference from static
   electrical charges. When electrical bonding is used, observe the following:

   1. Keep bonding jumpers as short as possible.
   2. Prepare bonded surfaces for best contact (resistance of connections should not exceed
   0.003 ohm).

   3. Avoid use of solder to attach bonding jumpers. Clamps and screws are preferred.
   4. For bonding aluminum alloy, use aluminum alloy or tinned, or cadmium-plated
   copper jumpers. Use brass or bronze jumpers on steel parts.
   5. When contact between dissimilar metals cannot be avoided, put a protective coating

   3.9 Electrical Load Analysis Procedure.

 3.9.1 Available Power Supply. To preclude overloading the electric power system of the
   aircraft when additional equipment is added, perform an electrical load analysis to
   determine whether the available power is adequate. Radio equipment must operate
   satisfactorily throughout the voltage range of the aircraft electrical system under taxi,
   takeoff, slow cruise, normal cruise, and landing operating conditions. Compute the
   electrical load analysis for the most adverse operating conditions; typically, this is for
   night and/or instrument flight.

 3.9.2 ASTM Method. One method for the analysis may be found in the ASTM International
   F2490-20, Standard Guide for Aircraft Electrical Load and Power Source Capacity
   Analysis.

 3.9.3 Previous Electrical Load Analysis. Applicable elements of a previously performed
   electrical load analysis if available may be reused.

 3.11 Functional Hazard Assessment (FHA).

   Note: Refer to AC 23.1309-1 for additional information on FHA.

3.11.1 Many older aircraft designs did not provide for all weather operations or potential
   increases in the pilot’s reliance on installed systems and equipment. Requirements to
   ensure design safeguards against hazards have developed as technologies have become
   available and pilots have increased their reliance on installed systems and equipment. A
   fundamental analysis to ensure design safeguards is accomplished through an FHA.

3.11.2 Alterations that involve systems or equipment that perform critical functions or that
   include complex designs with a high degree of integration, use of new technology, or
   novel applications of conventional technology must be assessed to determine the severity
   of failure conditions. Complexity in itself does not drive the need to perform a system
   safety analysis, but the effect of a failure does. Comparison with similar previously
   approved systems is sometimes helpful.

3.11.3 Evaluate the system to determine whether it is essential to safe operation. For aircraft of
   6,000 pounds or less maximum weight, refer to the regulations incorporated by reference
   in the type certificate (TC), unless the Administrator has found that the change is
   significant in an area.

3.11.4 Determine whether the equipment has any unacceptable adverse effects when operated.

3.11.5 Determine whether the operation of the installed equipment has an adverse effect on
   equipment that is not essential to safe operation, and whether a means exists to inform the
   pilot of the effect.

3.11.6 Determine whether a failure or malfunction of the installed equipment could result in
   unacceptable hazards.

3.11.7 Design requirements and the methodology of hazard resolution differ depending on the
   application and type of aircraft (i.e., single-engine, multiengine, and commuter use).

   3.11.7.1  It is unacceptable to operate equipment that has an adverse effect on other
   equipment essential to the safe operation of the aircraft.

   3.11.7.2  Operation of equipment that has an adverse effect on other equipment that is
   not essential to the safe operation of the aircraft may be acceptable if there is a
   means to inform the pilot of the effect.

   3.11.7.3  It is unacceptable if a probable failure or malfunction will result in a hazard in
   a multiengine aircraft.

   3.11.7.4  If a probable failure or malfunction will result in a hazard in a single-engine
   aircraft, its impact must be minimized.
