   CHAPTER 8. ROTORCRAFT EXTERNAL-LOAD DEVICE INSTALLATIONS,
   CARGO SLINGS, AND EXTERNAL RACKS

   Section 1. General

 8.1 Purpose. This section contains structural and design information for the fabrication and
   installation of a cargo sling used as an external-load attaching means for a Class B
   rotorcraft-load combination operation under 14 CFR part 133. As an external-load
   attaching means, a “cargo sling” includes a quick-release device (hook) and the
   associated cables, fittings, etc., used for the attachment of the cargo sling to the rotorcraft.
   Title 14 CFR § 133.43(b) specifies the requirements for the quick-release device.

 8.2 Hazards and Warnings. Particular attention should be paid to the effect of the sling load
   on the lateral as well as the fore and aft center of gravity (CG) of the rotorcraft. Use type
   certificate (TC), Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA), TS0, or Designated Engineering
   Representative (DER) approved parts.

 8.3 References. It is the installer’s responsibility to ensure that the latest revisions of any
   regulations, ACs, manufacturer’s data, etc., are used in any cargo sling or rack
   installation. Refer to 14 CFR parts 27 and 29; Civil Air Regulation (CAR) 6, Rotorcraft
   Airworthiness; Normal Category; and CAR 7, Rotorcraft Airworthiness; Transport
   Categories.

 8.4 Installation Considerations.

8.4.1 Location of Cargo Release in Relation to the Rotorcraft’s CG Limits. An ideal location
   would be one that allows the line of action to pass through the rotorcraft’s CG at all times
   (see Figure 8-1, Location of Cargo Release in Relation to the Rotorcraft’s Center of
   Gravity, Illustration A). However, with most cargo sling installations, this ideal situation
   is realized only when the line of action is vertical or near vertical and through the
   rotorcraft’s CG (see Figure 8-1, Illustration B).

   8.4.1.1  Whenever the line of action does not pass through the rotorcraft’s CG due to
   the attachment method used, acceleration forces, or aerodynamic forces, the
   rotorcraft-load combined CG will shift from the rotorcraft’s CG position.
   Depending upon the factors involved, the shift may occur along either or both
   the longitudinal or lateral axes. The amount of shift is dependent upon the
   force applied (F) and the length of the arm of the line of action. Their product
   (F x Arm) yields a moment that can be used to determine the rotorcraft-load
   combined CG (see Figure 8-1, Illustration C). If the rotorcraft-load CG is
   allowed to shift beyond the rotorcraft’s approved CG limits, the rotorcraft may
   become violently uncontrollable.

   8.4.1.2  Thus, any attachment method or location that decreases the length of the arm

   and line of action angle (see Figure 8-1, Illustration D).

8.4.2 Maximum External-Load. The maximum external-load (including the weight of the cargo
   sling) for which authorization is requested may not exceed the rated capacity of the
   quick-release device.

 8.5 Fabrication and Installation.

8.5.1 Static Test. The cargo sling installation must be able to withstand the static load required
   by 14 CFR §§ 27.865(a) and 29.865(a). Conduct the test as outlined in Chapter 2,
   Structural Data. If required during the test, supports may be placed at the landing gear to
   airframe attach fittings to prevent detrimental deformation of the landing gear due to the
   weight of the aircraft.

8.5.2 Sling-Leg Angles of Cable-Supported Slings. The optimum sling-leg angle (measured
   from the horizontal) is 45 to 60 degrees. Minimum tension in a sling leg occurs with a
   sling-leg angle of 90 degrees, and the tension approaches infinity as the angle approaches
   zero. Thus, larger sling-leg angles are desirable from a standpoint of cable strength
   requirements. Slings should not be attached in such a manner as to provide sling-leg
   angles of less than 30 degrees.

8.5.3 Minimum Sling-Leg Cable Strength.

   8.5.3.1  An analysis that considered the effects of 30-degree sling angles showed that
   the minimum cable strength design factor required would be 2.5 times the
   maximum external load for each leg regardless of the number of legs.
   Although this is the minimum strength required by 14 CFR part 133, it may be
   desirable to double this value to allow for the deterioration of the sling-leg
   cables in service. This will result in a cable strength equal to five times the
   maximum external load.

   Example: Maximum external load 850 pounds
   Minimum required sling-leg cable
   strength 850 x 2.5 = 2,125
   Minimum desired sling-leg cable
   strength 850 x 2.5 x 2 = 4,250

   8.5.3.2  A 3/16-inch, nonflexible 19-wire cable (MIL-DTL-87161G) provides a

8.5.4 Sling Installation.

   8.5.4.1  Attach the cargo sling to landing gear members or other structure capable of
   supporting the loads to be carried. Install the quick-release device in a level
   attitude with the throat opening facing the direction as indicated on the
   quick-release device. When cables are used to support the quick-release
   device, make sure the cables are not twisted or allowed to twist in the
   direction to unlay the cable.

   8.5.4.2  Some cargo release devices are provided with a fitting to permit installation of
   a guideline to assist in fully automatic engagement of the load target ring or
   load bridle. Secure the guideline to the quick-release device with a shear pin
   of a definite known value that will shear if a load becomes entangled on or
   over the guideline. Provision should also be made for cable-supported slings
   to be drawn up against the fuselage into a stowage position to prevent striking
   or dragging the release on the ground when not in use.

8.5.5 Installation of Release Controls. See Figure 8-2, Typical Cargo Sling Wiring Diagram,
   for a typical wiring diagram of the electrical controls.

   8.5.5.1  Install a cargo release master switch, readily accessible to the pilot, to provide
   a means of deactivating the release circuit. The power for the electrical release
   circuit should originate at the primary bus. The “auto” position of the release
   master switch on some cargo release units provides for automatic release
   when the load contacts the ground and the load on the release is reduced to a
   preset value.

   8.5.5.2  Install the cargo release switch on one of the pilot’s primary controls. It is
   usually installed on the cyclic stick to allow the pilot to release the load with
   minimum distraction after maneuvering the load into the release position.

   8.5.5.3  Install the emergency manual release control in a suitable position that is
   readily accessible to the pilot or other crewmember. Allow sufficient slack in
   the control cable to permit complete cargo movement without tripping the
   cargo release.

   8.5.5.4  The manual ground release handle, a feature of some cargo release units,
   permits ground personnel to open the cargo release.

   8.5.5.5  Label or placard all release controls as to each function and operation.

8.5.6 Functional Test.

   8.5.6.1  Test the release action of each quick-release device release control with
   various loads up to and including the maximum external load. If the necessary
   load can be applied, this may be done in a test fixture or while installed on the
   rotorcraft.

8.5.7 Supplemental Flight Information. The aircraft may not be used in 14 CFR part 133
   external-load operations until a Rotorcraft-Load Combination Flight Manual (RLCFM) is
   prepared in accordance with 14 CFR § 133.47. Appropriate entries should also be made
   in the aircraft’s Weight and Balance (W&B) data, equipment list, and logbooks. The
   FAA Form 337, Major Repair and Alteration (Airframe, Powerplant, Propeller, or
   Appliance), should also be executed as required by 14 CFR § 43.5(a) and (b).

8.5.8 Inspection and Maintenance. Inspection of the complete installation should be
   accomplished prior to each lift for security and functionality. Maintenance should be
   accomplished in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

   Section 2. Cargo Racks

 8.6 General. This section contains structural and design information for the fabrication and
   installation of a cargo rack used as an external-load attaching means for a Class A
   rotorcraft-load combination operation under 14 CFR part 133.

 8.7 Fabrication of Cargo Racks. The type of construction and method of attachment depend
   upon the material to be used and the configuration of the rotorcraft involved.
   Figures 8-10 through 8-14, Typical Cargo Rack Installation No. 3, illustrate typical
   construction and installation methods.

   Figure 8-10. Typical Cargo Rack Installation No. 1

 8.8 Static Test. The cargo rack installation must withstand the static test load required by
   14 CFR § 27.865(a). Conduct the test as outlined in Chapter 2.

 8.9 Supplemental Flight Information. The aircraft may not be used in 14 CFR part 133
   external-load operations until an RLCFM is prepared in accordance with 14 CFR
   § 133.47.
