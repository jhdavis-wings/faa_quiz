   CHAPTER 7. OXYGEN SYSTEM INSTALLATIONS IN NONPRESSURIZED
   AIRCRAFT

   Section 1. General

 7.1 Purpose. This chapter provides data for acceptable means of gaseous oxygen system
   installations in nonpressurized aircraft. For other oxygen system installations (e.g., liquid
   oxygen), installers should contact the responsible Flight Standards District Office
   (FSDO) for assistance in applying for a Supplemental Type Certificate (STC).

 7.2 Hazards and Warnings to Consider When Installing an Oxygen System.

7.2.1 Oxygen itself does not burn, but materials that burn in air will burn much hotter and more
   vigorously in an oxygen-rich environment.

7.2.2 Oil and grease burn with explosive violence in the presence of oxygen.

7.2.3 Rapid release of high-pressure oxygen in the presence of foreign particles can cause
   temperatures sufficient to ignite combustible materials and materials that would not
   normally burn in air.

7.2.4 Pressurized oxygen cylinder failures, particularly aluminum-lined composite cylinders,
   have the potential of producing violent explosions.

   Section 2. Installation of the Oxygen System

 7.4 System Requirements. Gaseous oxygen systems may be a higher-pressure system with
   oxygen stored at 1,850 pounds per square inch (psi) or a low-pressure system with the
   oxygen stored at 425 psi. All oxygen systems contain a storage tank, a regulation system,
   and a distribution system. The main difference in system type is in the regulation of the
   oxygen to the user (see paragraph 7.4.6 below).

7.4.1 Cylinders. Install oxygen cylinders conforming to Interstate Commerce Commission
   (ICC) requirements for gas cylinders that carry the ICC or Department of Transportation
   (DOT) 3A, 3AA, or 3HT designation followed by the service pressure metal-stamp on
   the cylinder.

7.4.2 Tubing/Lines.

   7.4.2.1  In systems with low pressure, use seamless aluminum alloy or equivalent,
   with having an outside diameter of 5/16 inch and a wall thickness of
   0.035 inch. Double flare the ends to attach to fittings.

   7.4.2.2  In high-pressure systems (1,800 psi), use 3/16-inch outside diameter (O.D.),
   0.035-inch wall thickness, seamless copper alloy tubing meeting Specification
   WWT-779 (as revised) type N, or stainless steel between the filler valve and
   the pressure-reducing valve. Depending on the process used, silver-solder
   cone nipples to the ends of the tubing in accordance with American Welding
   Society Specification C3.4, C3.5, C3.6, or C3.7.

   7.4.2.3  Use 5/16-inch O.D. aluminum alloy tubing after the pressure-reducer
   (low-pressure side).

   Note 1: Any lines that pass through potential fire zones should be
   stainless steel.

   Note 2: If lines are located behind upholstery or are not 100 percent
   visible during normal operations, they should be solid metal lines or
   high-pressure flexible lines.

7.4.3 Fittings. All fittings must be manufactured from materials that are compatible for use
   with oxygen systems. Fittings should not be made of mild steel or materials that are prone
   to corrosion when in contact with another material.

   7.4.3.1  High Pressure. Intercylinder connections are made with regular flared or
   flareless tube fittings made of stainless steel. Usually, fittings are of the same
   material as the lines. Mild steel or aluminum alloy fittings with stainless steel
   lines are discouraged. Titanium fittings should never be used because of a
   possible chemical reaction and resulting fire.

   7.4.3.2  Low Pressure. Fittings for metallic low-pressure lines are flared or flareless,
   similar to high-pressure lines. Line assemblies should be terminated with

   “B” nuts in a similar manner to a manufactured terminating connection.
   Universal adapters (AN-807) or friction nipples used in conjunction with hose
   clamps are not acceptable for use in pressurized oxygen systems.

7.4.4 Valves. Each system must contain a slow opening or closing shutoff valve that is
   accessible to a flight crewmember to turn on and shut off the oxygen supply at the high-
   pressure source.

7.4.5 Regulators. The cylinder or system pressure is reduced to the individual cabin outlets by
   means of a pressure-reducing regulator that can be manually or automatically controlled.
   The regulator should be mounted as close as possible to the cylinders and certificated for
   aviation environment.

7.4.6 Types of Regulators. The four basic types of oxygen systems, classified according to the
   type of regulator employed, are:

   7.4.6.1  Continuous-Flow Type. The constant flow type provides the same output
   pressure or flow regardless of altitude. Continuous-flow oxygen systems
   provide protection for passengers up to 25,000 feet mean sea level (MSL) and
   a continuous flow of 100 percent oxygen to the user. They may be automatic
   or manual in their operation.

   7.4.6.2  Diluter-Demand Type. This type dilutes pure oxygen with ambient air and
   maintains the proper portion of oxygen in the breathing gas depending on
   altitude. Oxygen concentration is automatically diluted proportionate to the
   specific altitude’s predetermined human oxygen consumption requirement.
   Such systems only supply oxygen, mixed with cabin air, during inhalation.

   7.4.6.3  Demand Type. This type uses high-pressure compressed oxygen that feeds a
   supply of oxygen to a “high-to-low” pressure regulator at the individual crew
   station. The regulator, after reducing the higher pressure, automatically cycles
   low-pressure breathing oxygen to the wearer only on demand. Title 14 CFR
   § 23.1441 requires aircraft that are certified to operate above 25,000 feet to be
   equipped with a demand system to supply required crewmembers.

   7.4.6.4  Pressure-Demand Type. This type is not likely to be included on small
   general-aviation models. Aircraft certified to exceed 40,000 feet must have a
   pressure-demand system, which delivers pressurized oxygen to pilots, such as
   a high-altitude oxygen breathing system (above 40,000 feet) using
   anthropomorphic facial measurements of aircrew to produce a mask that
   would satisfactorily contain the pressure required to allow breathing under
   pressure while maintaining an airtight face-seal and also remaining relatively
   comfortable to the wearer.

 7.4.7 Flow Indicators.

   7.4.7.1  A pith-ball flow indicator, vane, wheel anemometer, or lateral pressure
   indicator that fluctuates with changes in flow, or any other satisfactory flow
   indicator may be used in a continuous-flow type system.

   7.4.7.2  An Air Force/Navy (AN) flow indicator or equivalent may be used in a
   diluter-demand type system. Each flow indicator should give a positive
   indication when oxygen flow is occurring.

 7.4.8 Relief Valve.

   7.4.8.1  A relief valve is installed in low-pressure oxygen systems to safely relieve
   excessive pressure, such as that caused by overcharging.

   7.4.8.2  A relief valve is installed in high-pressure oxygen systems to safely relieve
   excessive pressure, such as that caused by heating.

 7.4.9 Gauge. Provide a pressure gauge to show the amount of oxygen in the cylinder during
   flight.

7.4.10 Masks. Only approved masks designed for the particular system should be used.

  7.5 Installation and Design Considerations. Oxygen systems present a hazard. Therefore,
   follow the precautions and practices listed below.

 7.5.1 Before working with oxygen equipment, remove oil, grease (including lip salves, hair
   oil, etc.), and dirt from hands, clothing, and tools.

 7.5.2 Prior to cutting the upholstery, check the intended route of the system.

   CAUTION: Ensure all system components are kept completely free of oil or
   grease during installation and locate components so they will not contact or
   become contaminated by oil or oil lines.

 7.5.3 Keep open ends of cleaned and dried tubing capped or plugged at all times, except during
   attachment or detachment of parts. Do not use tape, rags, or paper.

 7.5.4 Clean all lines and fittings that have not been cleaned and sealed by one of the following
   methods:

   7.5.4.1  A vapor-degreasing method with stabilized trichloroethylene conforming to
   Specification MIL-T-7003 or carbon tetrachloride. Blow tubing clean and dry
   with a stream of clean, dried, water-pumped air or dry nitrogen (water-vapor
   content of less than 0.005 milligrams per liter of gas at 700 ºF and
   760 millimeters of mercury pressure).

   7.5.4.2  Flush with naphtha conforming to Specification TT-N-95B; blow clean and
   dry off all solvent with water-pumped air; flush with anti-icing fluid
   conforming to Specification TT-1-735A or anhydrous ethyl alcohol; rinse
   thoroughly with fresh water; and dry thoroughly with a stream of clean, dried,
   water-pumped air or by heating at a temperature of 250 to 300 ºF for a half
   hour.

   7.5.4.3  Flush with hot inhibited alkaline cleaner until free from oil and grease. Rinse
   thoroughly with fresh water, and dry thoroughly with a stream of clean, dried,
   water-pumped air or by heating at a temperature of 250 to 300 ºF for a half an
   hour.

 7.5.5 Install lines, fittings, and equipment above and at least 6 inches away from fuel, oil, and
   hydraulic systems. Use deflector plates where necessary to keep hydraulic fluids away
   from the lines, fittings, and equipment.

 7.5.6 Allow at least a 2-inch clearance between the plumbing and any flexible control cable or
   other flexible moving parts of the aircraft. Provide at least a 1/2-inch clearance between
   the plumbing and any rigid control tubes or other rigid moving parts of the aircraft.

 7.5.7 Allow a 6-inch separation between the plumbing and the flight and engine control cables
   and electrical lines. When electrical conduit is used, this separation between the plumbing
   and conduit may be reduced to 2 inches.

 7.5.8 Route the oxygen system tubing, fittings, and equipment away from hot air ducts and
   equipment. Insulate or provide space between these items to prevent heating the oxygen
   system.

 7.5.9 Mount all plumbing in a manner that prevents vibration and chafing. Support a
   3/16-inch O.D. copper line each 24 inches and a 3/16-inch O.D. aluminum each 36 inches
   with cushioned loop-type line support clamps (AN-742) or equivalent.

7.5.10 Locate the oxygen supply valve (control valve) to allow its operation by the pilot during
   flight. The cylinder shutoff valve may be used as the supply control valve if it is operable
   from the pilot’s seat. Manifold plug-in type outlets, which are incorporated in automatic
   systems, may be considered oxygen supply valves since the pilot can control the flow of
   oxygen by engaging and disengaging the plug-in type of oxygen mask.

7.5.11 Filler connections, if provided, are recommended to be located outside the fuselage skin
   or isolated in a manner that would prevent leaking oxygen from entering the aircraft. Any
   nearby source of fuel, oil, or hydraulic fuel should be carefully evaluated under normal or
   malfunction conditions. Each filler connection should be placarded. Additionally, any
   valve (aircraft or ground servicing equipment) associated with high pressure should be
   slow acting.

   Note: Locate the oxygen shutoff valve on or as close as practicable to the cylinder
   to prevent loss of oxygen due to leakage in the system.

 7.6 Equipment Location and Mounting. Prior to commencing the installation, determine
   the weight/load factors and center of gravity (CG) limits.

7.6.1 Mount the cylinder in the baggage compartment or other suitable location in such a
   position that the shutoff valve is readily accessible. Provide access to this valve from
   inside the cabin so that it may be turned on or off in flight.

7.6.2 Fasten the cylinder brackets securely to the aircraft, preferably to a frame member or
   floorboard using AN bolts with fiber or similar locking nuts. Add sufficient plates,
   gussets, stringers, cross-bracing, or other reinforcements, where necessary, to provide a
   mounting that will withstand the inertia forces, stipulated in Chapter 2, Structural Data.

7.6.3 When cylinders are located where baggage or stored materials may damage them, protect
   them with a suitable guard or covering.

7.6.4 Provide at least 1/2-inch of clear airspace between any cylinder and a firewall or shroud
   isolating a designated fire zone.

7.6.5 Mount the regulator close to the cylinder to avoid long high-pressure lines.

7.6.6 Store the masks so that there will be a minimum delay in removing and putting them into
   use.

 7.7 Thread Compound. Use anti-seize or thread-sealing compound conforming to
   Specification MIL-PRF-27617F or equivalent.

7.7.1 Do not use compound on aluminum alloy flared tube fittings having straight threads.
   Proper flaring and tightening should be sufficient to make a flared tube connection
   leakproof.

7.7.2 Treat all male-tapered pipe threads with antiseize and sealing compound
   (MIL-PRF-27617F, or polytetrafluorethylene tape A-A-58092) or equivalent.

7.7.3 Apply the compound in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendation. Make sure
   that the compounds are carefully and sparingly applied only to male threads, coating the
   first three threads from the end of the fitting. Do not use compound on the coupling
   sleeves or on the outside of the tube flares.

 7.8 Functional Test. Before inspection plates, cover plates, or upholstering are replaced,
   make a system check that includes at least the following:

7.8.1 Open the cylinder valve slowly and observe the pressure gauge.

7.8.2 Open the supply valve and remove one of the mask tubes and bayonet fittings from one
   of the masks in the kit. Plug the bayonet into each of the oxygen outlets. A small flow
   should be noted from each of the outlets. This can be detected by holding the tube to the
   lips while the bayonet is plugged into an outlet.

7.8.3 Check the complete system for leaks. This can be done with a soap solution made only
   from mild (castile) soap or by leak-detector solution supplied by the oxygen equipment
   manufacturer.

7.8.4 If leaks are found, close the cylinder shutoff valve and reduce the pressure in the system
   by plugging a mask tube into one of the outlets or by carefully loosening one of the
   connections in the system. When the pressure has been reduced to zero, make the
   necessary repairs. Repeat the procedure until no leaks are found in the system.

   WARNING: Never tighten oxygen system fittings with oxygen pressure applied.

7.8.5 Test each outlet for leaks at the point where the mask tube plugs in. This can be done by
   using a soapy solution over each of the outlets. Use the solution sparingly to prevent
   clogging the outlet with soap. Remove all residue to prevent the accumulation of dirt.

7.8.6 Examine the system to determine if the flow of oxygen through each outlet is at least
   equal to the minimum required by the pertinent requirements at all altitudes at which the
   aircraft is operated. This can be accomplished by one of the following methods:

   7.8.6.1  In a continuous-flow system, when the calibration (inlet pressure versus flow)
   of the orifices used at the plug-in outlets is known, the pressure in the
   low-pressure distribution line can be measured at the point subject to the
   greatest pressure drop. Do this with oxygen flowing from all outlets. The
   pressure thus measured should indicate a flow equal to or greater than the
   minimum flow required.

   7.8.6.2  In lieu of the above procedure, the flow of oxygen through the outlet that is
   subject to the greatest pressure drop may be measured with all other outlets
   open. Gas meters, rotameters, or other suitable means may be used to measure
   flows.

   7.8.6.3  The measurement of oxygen flow in a continuous-flow system that uses a
   manually adjusted regulator can be accomplished at sea level. However, in a
   continuous-flow system that uses an automatic-type regulator, it may be
   necessary to check the flow at the maximum altitude that will be encountered
   during the aircraft’s normal operation. The manufacturer of the particular
   continuous-flow regulator used should be able to furnish data on the operating
   characteristics of the regulator from which it can be determined if a flight
   check is necessary.

   7.8.6.4  Checking the amount of flow through the various outlets in a diluter-demand
   or straight-demand system is not necessary since the flow characteristics of
   the particular regulator being used may be obtained from the manufacturer of
   the regulator. However, in such systems, the availability of oxygen to each
   regulator should be checked by turning the lever of the diluter-demand
   regulator to the “100 percent oxygen” position and inhaling through the tube
   via the mask to determine whether the regulator valve and the flow indicator
   are operating.

   Section 3. Airworthiness Compliance Check Sheet: Oxygen System Installation in
   Unpressurized Aircraft

 7.10 General. Oxygen system installations made in accordance with an FAA-approved
   airframe manufacturer or other FAA-approved installations may be accepted without
   further investigation. On other installations, the following points should be checked to
   determine if the installation is satisfactory.

 7.11 Applicable Federal Aviation Regulations. Determine if the installation complies with
   the regulations listed in paragraph 7.3.

 7.12 Structural Requirements.

7.12.1 If changes or alterations of the aircraft structure are made (mounting of oxygen cylinder),
   determine if the original strength and integrity of the structure is retained.

7.12.2 If gages are added to the instrument panel or an outside filler valve is installed on the
   fuselage, determine if the structural integrity of the panel and airframe or its supporting
   structure is retained.

7.12.3 Determine if the extent of the modification has affected the CG of the aircraft evaluated.

7.12.4 Check whether all lines are properly routed and supported.

 7.13 Hazards to the Aircraft or its Occupants. Determine if the design of the oxygen system
   is evaluated to ensure that the aircraft and its occupants are safe from hazards identified
   in paragraph 7.2.

 7.14 Operating Aspects.

7.14.1 When required by the operating rules for the use of supplemental oxygen, determine if
   the system’s capacity is sufficient to supply oxygen to all combinations of crew and
   passengers.

7.14.2 Determine if the oxygen system provides the required flow.

7.14.3 Determine if the oxygen regulator controls are accessible to a member of the flightcrew
   in flight.

7.14.4 Determine if there is a means, readily available to the crew in flight, to turn on and shut
   off the oxygen supply at the high-pressure source.

7.14.5 Check whether there is a means to allow the crew to readily determine during flight the
   quantity of oxygen available in each source of supply.

7.15 Detail Design Standards. Determine if:

   1. All parts are suitable for use with oxygen.
   2. The regulator is located as close as physically possible to the oxygen cylinder and
   minimizes the use of fittings.
   3. No solid metal lines are 100 percent visible during normal operation.
   4. Each breathing device has a device attached that visually shows the flow of oxygen.
   5. The lines that pass through potential fire zones are stainless steel.
   6. When oxygen system components are added to the baggage compartments, there are
   provisions to protect the system components from shifting cargo.
   7. Where oxygen components are installed, the compartment is placarded against the
   storage of oil or hydrocarbons.
   8. A smoke detector is installed where oxygen cylinders are installed in a closed,
   nonaccessible compartment.
   9. The cargo area weight limitations placard is updated.
   10. All oxygen outlets are placarded.
   11. “No Smoking When Oxygen Is In Use” placards and other appropriate placards
   (i.e., operation instructions) are visible to the crew and occupants of the aircraft.

7.16 Instructions for Continued Airworthiness (ICA). Determine if:

   1. There are written instructions concerning system operation, maintenance, and
   cylinder changing procedures.
   2. There are written instructions in the use of the oxygen equipment in the Aircraft
   Flight Manual (AFM) or placards.

   Note: Any changes to the AFM must be approved by the FAA.

   3. There are written inspection and test schedules and procedures.
   4. Mechanical drawings and wiring diagrams are available, as required.
   5. Written instructions are available concerning oxygen cylinder hydrostatic testing
   requirements and cylinder replacement requirements.
   6. There is a scheduled (annual) check of a constant flow system manifold output
   pressure for recommended output pressure.
   7. The guidance contained in AC 43.13-1, Chapter 9, Aircraft Systems and Components,
   paragraphs 9-47 through 9-51 is considered in the design of the oxygen maintenance
   program.
