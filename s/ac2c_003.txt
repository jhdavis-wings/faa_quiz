   CHAPTER 2. STRUCTURAL DATA

 2.1 General. Structural integrity is a major factor in aircraft design and construction.
   Addition or removal of equipment involving changes in weight could affect the structural
   integrity, weight, balance, flight characteristics, reliability, or performance of an aircraft.
   This chapter is generic in nature and meant to assist the aviation maintenance technician
   in determining structural integrity. It is not meant to circumvent utilizing an FAA
   engineer or the responsible Aircraft Certification Service office when necessary.

 2.2 Structural Design Process. Structural design processes follow these steps:

   •  Determine the overall load factors.
   •  Estimate the resulting loads.
   •  Distribute these loads over the aircraft.
   •  Determine the material, size, and shape of the part.
   •  Calculate the resulting stresses in the part.
   •  Compare these stresses with the maximum allowable for the material used.
   •  Resize the part as necessary.

 2.3 Types of Loads and Stresses.

2.3.1 “Limit load factors” are the maximum load factors that may be expected during service
   (the maneuvering, gust, or ground load factors established by the manufacturer for type
   certification).

2.3.2 Aircraft parts may be formed out of different types of material and joined together. Each
   of those parts carries a load, and the fastener that brings these parts together has to carry
   the load from one part to the other.

2.3.3 Every aircraft is subject to different types of structural stress. Stress acts on an aircraft
   whether it is on the ground or in flight. Stress is defined as a load applied to a unit area of
   material.

2.3.4 “Tension” is a force acting against another force that is trying to pull something apart.

2.3.5 “Compression” is a squeezing or crushing force that tries to make parts smaller.

2.3.6 “Torsion” is a twisting force.

2.3.7 “Shear stress” is when one piece of material slides over another.

2.3.8 “Bending” is a combination of two forces, compression, and tension. During bending
   stress, the material on the inside of the bend is compressed and the outside material is
   stretched in tension.

2.3.9 An aircraft structure in flight is subjected to variable stresses due to the varying loads that
   may be imposed. The designer’s problem involves anticipating the possible stresses that
   the structure will have to endure and build the structure strong enough to withstand these
   stresses.

 2.4 Static Loads. “Static loads” are loads that do not undergo change in magnitude or
   direction during a measurement procedure. Load factors are defined as follows:

2.4.1 “Limit load factors” are the maximum load factors that may be expected during service
   (the maneuvering, gust, or ground load factors established for type certification).

2.4.2 “Ultimate load factors” are the limit load factors multiplied by a prescribed factor of
   safety. Certain loads, such as the minimum ultimate inertia forces prescribed for
   emergency landing conditions, are given directly in terms of ultimate loads.

2.4.3 “Static test load factors” are the ultimate loads multiplied by the casting, fitting, bearing,
   and/or other special factors, when applicable. Where no special factors apply, the static
   test loads are equal to the ultimate loads.

2.4.4 “Critical static test load factors” are the greater of the maneuvering, gust, ground, and
   inertia load static test load factors for each direction (up, down, starboard, port, fore,
   and aft).

2.5.3 When an additional load is to be added to a structure already supporting previously
   installed equipment, determine the capability of the structure to support the total load
   (previous load plus added load). If the additional load requires access to applicable design
   data or the capability to reverse engineer the installation, further assistance may be
   required from FAA engineering, the responsible Aircraft Certification Service office, or
   an appropriately rated Designated Engineering Representative (DER).

 2.6 Static Tests.

   CAUTION: The aircraft or equipment, or both, can be damaged in applying static
   loads, particularly if a careless or improper procedure is used. It is recommended,
   whenever practicable, that static testing be conducted on a duplicate installation in
   a jig or mockup that simulates the related aircraft structure. Static test loads may
   exceed the yield limits of the assemblies being substantiated and can result in
   partially sheared fasteners, elongated holes, or other damage that may not be
   visible unless the structure is disassembled. If the structure is materially weakened
   during testing, it may fail at a later date. Riveted sheet metal and composite
   laminate construction methods especially do not lend themselves to easy detection
   of such damage. To conduct static tests:

2.6.1 Determine the weight and center of gravity (CG) position of the equipment item.

2.6.2 Install the attachment in the aircraft or, preferably, in a jig using the applicable static test
   load factors.

2.6.3 Determine the critical ultimate load factors for the sideward, upward, downward, and
   forward directions. A hypothetical, which follows steps 1 through 4 below, pertains to the
   example in Figure 2-3, Hypothetical of Determining Static Test Loads.

   1. Convert the gust, maneuvering, and ground load factors obtained from the
   manufacturer, FAA engineer, or DER to determine the ultimate load factors. Unless
   otherwise specified in the airworthiness standards applicable to the aircraft, ultimate

   testing is not necessary.

   procedures, checks, and balances may be applied to mitigate any risks.

 2.7 Materials and Workmanship. Use materials conforming to an accepted government or
   industry standard such as Army/Navy and Air Force/Navy (AN) National Aerospace
   Standards (NAS), Technical Standard Orders (TSO), or Military Specifications
   (MIL-SPEC).

2.7.1 Suitability and durability of materials used for parts, the failure of which could adversely
   affect safety, must:

   •  Be established by experience or tests;
   •  Meet approved specifications that ensure the strength, and other properties, assumed
   in the design data; and
   •  Take into account the effects of environmental conditions, such as temperature and
   humidity, expected in service.

2.7.2 Workmanship must be of a high standard.

 2.8 Material Strength Properties and Design Values. Material strength properties must be
   based on enough tests of material meeting specifications to establish design values on a
   statistical basis, such as Metallic Materials Properties Development and Standardization
   (MMPDS), available at https://www.mmpds.org/. Design values must be chosen to
   minimize the probability of structural failure due to material variability. Except as
   provided in paragraph 2.8.5 below, compliance with this paragraph must be shown by
   selecting design values that ensure material strength with the following probabilities:

 2.8.1 Where applied loads are eventually distributed through a single member within an
   assembly, the failure of which would result in loss of structural integrity of the
   component―99 percent probability with 95 percent confidence.

 2.8.2 For redundant structure, in which the failure of individual elements would result in
   applied loads being safely distributed to other load carrying members―90 percent
   probability with 95 percent confidence.

 2.8.3 The effects of temperature on allowable stresses used for design in an essential
   component or structure must be considered where thermal effects are significant under
   normal operating conditions.

 2.8.4 The design of the structure must minimize the probability of catastrophic fatigue failure,
   particularly at points of stress concentration.

 2.8.5 Design values greater than the guaranteed minimums required by paragraph 2.8 may be
   used where only guaranteed minimum values are normally allowed (e.g., if a “premium
   selection” of the material is made in which a specimen of each item is tested before use to
   determine if the actual strength properties of that particular item are equal or exceed those
   used in design).

   2.9 Fasteners. Use hardware conforming to an accepted government or industry standard
   such as AN, NAS, TSO, or MIL-SPEC. Attach equipment in such a way that prevents
   loosening in service due to vibration.

 2.9.1 Each removable fastener must incorporate two retaining devices in case one retaining
   device should fail during flight operations.

 2.9.2 Fasteners and their locking devices must not be adversely affected by the environmental
   conditions associated with the particular installation.

 2.9.3 No self-locking nut may be used on any bolt subject to rotation in operation unless a
   nonfriction locking device is used in addition to the self-locking device.

 2.10 Protection of Structure. Provide protection against deterioration or loss of strength due
   to corrosion, abrasion, electrolytic action, or other causes. Each part of the structure must:

2.10.1 Be suitably protected against deterioration or loss of strength in service due to any cause,
   including:

   •  Weathering,
   •  Corrosion, and
   •  Abrasion.

2.10.2 Have adequate provisions for ventilation and drainage.

 2.11 Accessibility. Provide adequate provisions to permit close examination of equipment or
   adjacent parts of the aircraft that regularly require, among other services, inspection,
   adjustment, and lubrication. For each part that requires maintenance, inspection, or other
   servicing, appropriate means must be incorporated into the aircraft design to allow such
   servicing to be accomplished.

 2.12 Effects on Weight and Balance (W&B). Ensure that the altered aircraft can be operated
   within the weight and CG ranges listed in the FAA type certificate (TC), data sheet, or
   Aircraft Listing Aircraft Specification. When adding items of mass to the aircraft, the
   effect on the empty W&B should be considered and documented in the aircraft W&B.
   Determine that the altered aircraft will not exceed maximum gross weight. If applicable,
   correct the loading schedule to reflect the current loading procedure. Refer to
   AC 43.13-1, Acceptable Methods, Techniques, and Practices―Aircraft Inspection and
   Repair, for W&B computation procedures.

 2.13 Effects on Safe Operation. Install equipment in a manner that will not interfere with or
   adversely affect the safe operation of the aircraft (controls, navigation equipment
   operation, etc.).

2.13.1 The factor of safety prescribed in 14 CFR § 23.303 must be multiplied by the highest
   pertinent special factors of safety prescribed in 14 CFR §§ 23.621 through 23.625 for
   each part of the structure where strength is:

   •  Uncertain,
   •  Likely to deteriorate in service before normal replacement, or
   •  Subject to appreciable variability because of uncertainties in manufacturing processes
   or inspection methods.

2.13.2 Unless otherwise provided, a factor of safety of 1.5 must be used.

 2.14 Controls and Indicators. Locate and identify equipment controls and indicators so they
   can be operated and read from the appropriate crewmember position.

 2.15 Placarding. Label equipment requiring identification and, if necessary, placard
   operational instructions. Amend W&B information as required. Any required placards
   installed or required by an alteration must be added to the “Limitations” section of the
   instructions for continued airworthiness (ICA) to ensure that maintenance personnel will
   know if a required placard is missing in future inspections.
