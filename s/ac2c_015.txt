   CHAPTER 14. LARGER TIRE INSTALLATIONS

 14.1 Purpose. Many aircraft operating in harsh and/or rough terrain environments have
   obtained FAA approval for installation of wheel/tire combinations larger than originally
   certificated on small single-engine airplanes of less than 6,000 pounds gross weight.
   These larger wheel/tire combinations are commonly referred to as tundra tire
   modifications and better adapt airplanes to a particular operating flying environment. The
   information provided in this chapter can generally be applied to fixed gear airplanes less
   than 6,000 pounds gross takeoff weight when installing tires and wheel assemblies that
   do not exceed 130 percent of the standard tire and wheel combination that the aircraft
   was originally designed with.

 14.2 Hazards and Warnings. Persons operating aircraft with larger size (tundra) tire
   installations should be aware of the safety risks involved and take appropriate measures
   to reduce those risks. With a wide range of operating parameters, it is not always possible
   to predict the effects of vibration, harmonics, drag, loading, or other characteristics
   induced by aircraft operating with these configurations. Therefore, it is incumbent on the
   operator to understand the potential risks and take appropriate measures for safe
   operation of the aircraft.

14.2.1 The FAA published guidance in AC 23-17, Systems and Equipment Guide for
   Certification of Part 23 Airplanes and Airships, summarizing flight test results, possible
   safety hazards, and general information about certification of aircraft with larger tires.
   This information is useful for other installations, even though the test aircraft for the data
   collected was primarily a Piper PA-18.

14.2.2 Operation of airplanes with larger tires may expose the occupants to additional risks
   because there is evidence of increased structural failure of the landing gear or
   components on aircraft operating with these modifications. The additional weight and
   surface area of tires can impose additional ground and air loads on the airplane. Ground
   handling, taxiing, takeoff, and landing can place significant side loads and twisting
   moments to the landing gear and its attachment structure, which can cause hidden
   damage or cumulative damage, or both. In-service failure of main landing gear struts,
   wheels and attachment hardware have resulted in aircraft accidents and incidents with
   injury to the occupants of the airplane. Installation, rigging, periodic inspection, and
   maintenance of larger tires, wheels, landing gear, and their attaching parts are of utmost
   importance to safety. Consultation with maintenance technicians and operators
   experienced in installing and operating aircraft with larger tires is strongly recommended
   when considering any new installation or alteration of an existing aircraft with larger
   tires.

 14.4 Installation Considerations.

14.4.1 Determining Eligibility of an Airplane. Many aircraft have been approved to use larger
   tires through the original manufacturer, field approval, or Supplemental Type Certificate
   (STC) process. It is advisable to research the aircraft Type Certificate Data Sheet
   (TCDS), specification sheet, or FAA STC database available through the FAA website at
   https://www.faa.gov website to see if the aircraft in question already has approval for the
   size tire. The FAA should not field approve a wheel/tire/brake combination that is already
   approved via another method such as STC. If larger tires are not previously approved for
   your particular airplane, the guidance in this chapter can be used to approve tires and
   wheels that are not categorized a major design change. The information provided in this
   chapter is limited to fixed gear airplanes with a gross weight of 6,000 pounds or less.
   Prior to an aircraft modification, a conformity evaluation of the aircraft and review of the
   aircraft records for previous alterations and repairs should be accomplished to ensure
   there is no effect on the proposed alteration. This evaluation should include inspection of
   the aircraft wings, tail, and control surfaces for improper repair or alterations and proper
   rigging.

14.4.2 Identification of Approved Tires, Wheels, and Brakes. Only tires, wheels, and brakes of
   an FAA-approved type and load rating for aircraft may be utilized. Approved wheels,
   brakes, and tires will be marked using plates, placards, or other methods identifying the
   TSO number (TSO-C26d, Aircraft Wheels, Brakes and Wheel/Brake Assemblies for
   Parts 23, 27 and 29 Aircraft, wheels and brakes) (TSO-C62e tires), an aircraft
   component, accessory, equipment type certificate (TC) number, an airplane part number
   (for type certificated airplanes), or a Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA).

   14.4.3.1  Known limit landing load factor. Before installation, determine that the
   maximum limit load rating (L) of the wheels specified on its identification
   plate or placard is at least equal to the maximum static load on it (S) times the
   limit landing load factor (fl) previously determined from drop tests of the

14.4.4 Landing Gear Moment Reactions: Landing Gear Bending Moments. In order to avoid
   excessive bending moments on the landing gear and attachment structure, the axle height
   (y) measured from the bottom surface of the tire to the axle centerline, as indicated in the
   figure below, must not exceed 130 percent of the static rolling radius of the standard
   tire/wheel approved for the airplane when the tire is inflated to the maximum rated
   inflation pressure. The maximum rated tire pressure can be found stamped on the side of
   the tire or will be included in the servicing information for the aircraft. It is not
   appropriate to use oversize or “tundra” tires to determine the static rolling radius. If
   lower-than-maximum rated tire pressure is used on the proposed larger tire to meet this
   requirement then that becomes a limitation on the installation. For example, if the larger
   tire is rated at 30 pounds per square inch (psi) maximum and can only be inflated to
   26 psi to meet the axle height requirement, 26 psi becomes the new maximum rated
   inflation limitation and needs to be placarded as a limitation.

14.4.5 Field Experience. This chapter contains feedback from maintenance personnel and pilots
   with extensive operational experience in these types of installations. The information is
   for informational purposes to aid in understanding some aspects concerning installation
   of larger wheels, tires, and brakes. Larger tires usually require more effective braking to
   meet certification requirements. Typical installations involve replacing drum wheels and
   brake assemblies with more effective hydraulic wheel and brake assemblies or in the case

   14-3

===== PDF PAGE 189 =====

   of the latter, changing from a single puck to a double puck style caliper to increase brake
   effectiveness. While additional brake effectiveness is needed to meet certain certification
   standards, it has been found that too much brake can be detrimental. As tire size and
   brake effectiveness increase, so do the effects on the aircraft. For example, the larger
   footprint and dynamic force of the tire combined with more effective braking will result
   in the increased tendency for the aircraft to nose over. On conventional gear aircraft, it
   results in an increased chance of propeller strike and ground loop. On tricycle gear
   aircraft, it increases the stress on the forward landing gear structure to include the firewall
   and engine mount. Moving the forward center of gravity (CG) aft will reduce the stresses
   on the nose landing gear.

   14.4.5.1  When increasing tire size, it is recommended that the axle strength be
   increased to reduce in-service failures. For example, Piper model J-3 through
   PA-20 aircraft equipped with 1¼ inch axles are known to bend at the inner
   bearing stop. This has been prevented by pressing a Chrome-Moly sleeve
   inside the axle to reinforce it or by changing to the larger 1½ inch axles. On
   Cessna aircraft, it is recommended that the higher strength axles designed for
   ski installations should be installed.

   14.4.5.2  In addition, several other modifications are available to adapt aircraft to a
   harsher operating environment such as landing gear attach reinforcement kits,
   check cables, horizontal stabilizer leading edge abrasion boots, and gravel
   guards that have been born out of necessity to reduce damage and increase the
   longevity of aircraft while operating off airports.

14.4.6 Performing the Modification. The modification must be performed by appropriately
   certificated persons using aircraft quality parts in accordance with acceptable methods,
   techniques, and practices. ACs 23-17, 43-210, and 43.13-1, and manufactures’
   information are good sources of information to evaluate, perform, and document the
   larger tire installations. After the installation is complete, the equipment list and Weight
   and Balance (W&B) data need to be updated to reflect the new installation. The W&B
   can be accomplished by actual weighing or computation. A summary of considerations,
   not all inclusive, for approval of larger tire installations is provided and may be useful as
   a job aid.

 14.5 Flight and Ground Handling Operational Checks.

14.5.1 Characteristics. Accomplish an operational check in accordance with 14 CFR § 91.407(b)
   to determine the takeoff, landing, and ground handling characteristics. Verify that ground
   control is adequate to satisfactorily complete a landing run with a turnoff at slow speed.
   In flight, the larger tires must not produce excessive drag or undesirable flight
   characteristics. Where climb requirements are not critical, and the climb reduction is
   small (not exceeding 50 feet per minute), the FAA will accept a statement of the
   approximate reduction in climb performance placed in the performance information
   section of the Aircraft Flight Manual Supplement (AFMS). For larger variations in climb
   performance, where the minimum requirements are critical, appropriate climb data should

   AFMS. Enter a notation of this check-in the airplane records.

14.5.3 Potential Adverse Effects of Larger Tire Installations. Larger tire installations on
   airplanes may produce one or more of the following effects on handling characteristics:

   1. Reduced ability of brakes to hold against takeoff power.
   2. Reduced brake effectiveness during rejected takeoff and balked landing.
   3. Reduced stability and controllability during rejected or balked landing and go around.
   4. Change in either trim range or trim authority, or both.
   5. Reduced directional stability and control during takeoff and landing ground rolls,
   with increased tendency to ground loop.
   6. Increased tendency for conventional gear airplanes to nose over during landing on
   paved surfaces more than during landings on gravel, grass, or other surfaces that
   allow the tires to skid more easily.
   7. Reduced stall warning margin, change in either aerodynamic stall warning
   characteristics (warning buffet) or reduced effectiveness of stall warning system, or
   both, in both level and turning flight with power either on or off, or both.
   8. Changes in stalling and stall recovery behavior in both level and turning flight with
   power either on or off, or both. Stalls may become more abrupt and altitude loss
   before recovery may increase.
   9. Increased tendency to enter an inadvertent spin and reduced ability to recover from
   the spin.
   10. Reduced longitudinal, lateral, and directional stability.
   11. Increased airframe vibration and buffet.

14.5.4 Potential Propulsion Systems Effects.

   1. Fuel flow may be affected by changes in normal flying attitude.
   2. Unusable fuel may be affected by changes in normal flying attitude.
   3. The fuel tank sump may be affected by a change in ground attitude.
   4. Fuel drains may be affected by a change in ground attitude.
   5. Engine cooling may be affected by performance or flying attitude changes.

 14.6 Testing the Modification. After the modification has been installed and all necessary
   maintenance entries have been made for the approval for return to service, an operational
   maintenance check is required in accordance with 14 CFR § 91.407 to ensure that the
   installation functions as intended. The operational check should be performed as follows:

14.6.1 No passengers permitted during operational maintenance checks.

14.6.2 All flights conducted in accordance with the aircraft’s flight manual or limitations.

14.6.3 Prepare the aircraft for testing.

   1. The checks should be performed at maximum gross weight.
   2. Inflate tire to the minimum allowable inflation pressure for the airplane weight.
   3. Using white shoe polish or equivalent, coat the brake (inboard) side of tire sidewall. If
   larger nose tires are installed coat both sides of sidewall also.

14.6.4 Ground turning and braking tests for tire slippage and interference.

   1. Conduct at least two maximum effort non-skidding taxi turns to the right and left.
   2. With takeoff power applied, ensure that the brakes hold the aircraft static.
   3. Conduct a minimum of two short field landings using maximum braking at max gross
   operating weight.

14.6.5 Aircraft minimum weight: Flight(s) conducted at the most forward and at the most aft
   CG.

14.6.6 Aircraft at gross weight: Flight(s) conducted at the most forward and at the most aft CG.

14.6.7 After completion of operational flights, inspect the tire, wheel, and brake installation for
   evidence of abrasion contact on the sidewall of the tire and any other indication that the
   clearance between the installed components is insufficient. If all ground handling and
   performance checks are found satisfactory, the pilot will sign the aircraft’s airframe
   maintenance (log) book, certifying that the aircraft is safe to operate within the
   operational limits of the aircraft’s design. This certification must contain the pilot’s name,
   certificate number, and date the operational check was performed.

   Note: In the event operational performance is found to be below that established
   during certification, corrective action needs to be taken. On fixed landing gear
   landplanes, where climb requirements are not critical and the climb reduction is
   small (30 to 50 feet per minute), the FAA will accept a statement of the
   approximate reduction in climb performance placed in the performance

 14.7 Maintenance (Including Inspection).

14.7.1 Inspection and Repair Data Sources. Contact the airplane, wheel, brake, or tire
   manufacturers for any specific inspection and maintenance instructions they may have
   developed. Check the applicable directory for Airworthiness Directives (AD) for the
   products being used. Refer to AC 43.13-1, Chapter 9, Aircraft Systems and Components,
   for more information.

14.7.2 Instructions for Continued Airworthiness (ICA). The installer must provide instructions
   for future inspection, maintenance, and repair of the added or altered parts, and is also
   responsible for assessing the need for any changes to the product-level ICA (changes that
   affect the airplane as a whole when the tires are installed). For simple airplane/tire
   combinations where tires of the same size have been approved on similar airplanes with
   appropriate ICA, it may only be necessary to reference those ICA in the maintenance
   records of the newly altered airplane and/or in block 8 of the FAA Form 337, Major
   Repair and Alteration (Airframe, Powerplant, Propeller, or Appliance), documenting the
   larger tire installation. For complex tire installations requiring special considerations, the
   modifier may need to develop new installation-specific ICA. In either case, the modifier
   must ensure that adequate and appropriate ICA is available to the owner or operator of
   the modified aircraft. Refer to AC 43-210, Paragraph 5, Maintenance Information, for
   additional guidance regarding ICA. Operators intending to modify aircraft to these
   configurations should consider the following:

   14.7.2.1  Inspect the area of the fuselage attachment clamp area, axle attachment area,
   and the axle for cracks, corrosion, and any other defect that might induce
   failure. Performance of these inspections should be in accordance with “best
   practices” commonly used in industry with, at a minimum, the use of a dye
   penetrate method of Nondestructive Testing (NDT) with the aid of
   magnification or other methods, techniques, and practices acceptable to the
   FAA.

   their aircraft.

   14.8 Interchanging of Skis and Wheels.

 14.8.1 W&B Computation. A person appropriately authorized by 14 CFR § 43.3 must perform a
   new W&B computation when the larger tires are initially installed. The FAA
   recommends that the airplane be weighed for this initial computation. After the initial
   installation, removing the larger tires and/or brakes and returning the aircraft to its
   original configuration or vice versa is considered a preventive maintenance operation if it
   does not involve complex assembly operations or a new W&B computation. Refer to
   14 CFR part 43 appendix A, paragraph (c)(18).

   Note: During subsequent W&B changes to the airplane, be sure to update the
   W&B records and equipment list to ensure proper configuration of all approved
   ski, wheel, and float installations.

 14.8.2 Periodic Inspection Required. Seasonally removed and installed equipment items such as
   tires, skis and floats should be inspected at installation to comply with 14 CFR
   §§ 91.407(a) and 91.409(a), and 14 CFR part 43 appendix D, if they were not installed on
   the airplane at the time of the last inspection. All available data described in this
   paragraph should be used during the inspection.

   14.9 Documentation.

 14.9.1 Alterations. Modifications not listed in the aircraft specifications, incorporating larger
   tires, wheels, or brakes are considered a major alteration. Therefore, it is recommended
   that the procedures contained in ACs 43.9-1 and 43-210 be utilized to meet the recording
   requirements contained in 14 CFR part 43.

 14.9.2 Performance Information. The following paragraphs contain the minimum additional

 14.10 Summary of Considerations for Approval of Larger Tire Installation (may be used
   as a job aid).

14.10.1 If an STC exists, it must be used.

14.10.2 All tires must be either TSO or PMA approved.

14.10.3 Tire size must not exceed 35 x 15 inches.

14.10.4 Procedures:

   1. Review ACs 23-17 and 43-210.
   2. Review the aircraft records for previous alterations and repairs to ensure there is no
   effect on the proposed alteration.
   3. Conduct a conformity evaluation to ensure that the proposed alteration will not
   impact the airworthiness of the aircraft.
   4. Submit FAA Form 337 prepared in accordance with 14 CFR part 43 and ACs 43-210
   and 43.9-1, including the certification basis used for the field approval, a description
   of the proposed alteration, and ICA.
   5. The alteration must be performed by a certificated airframe mechanic, and
   accomplished using acceptable methods, techniques, and practices. Refer to 14 CFR
   §§ 43.7 and 43.13.
   6. Aircraft must be weighed with tires installed, and a new W&B generated. Refer to
   14 CFR §§ 43.13, 91.9, and 91.103.
   7. Aircraft CG must be determined for the most forward and aft conditions, at both
   empty and gross weights. Refer to AC 43.13-1, Chapter 10, Weight and Balance.
   8. The holder of an Inspection Authorization (IA) should inspect the aircraft for:
   •  Previous alterations or repairs that may not have been recorded.
   •  Compatibility of previous alterations or repairs.
   •  Airworthiness of the aircraft.

   14.10.4.1 Ensure that the aircraft wing, tail, and control surfaces have not been
   improperly altered and the aircraft is properly rigged (log book entry by a
   certified mechanic).

   14.10.4.2 Perform a series of maintenance operational check flights as follows:

   •  No passengers permitted.
   •  All flights conducted in accordance with the aircraft’s flight manual or
   limitations.
   •  Turning and braking tests for tire slippage and interference. Recommend
   conducting at minimum and maximum tire pressures.
   •  Aircraft minimum weight: Flight(s) conducted at the most forward and at
   the most aft CG.
   •  Aircraft at gross weight: Flight(s) conducted at the most forward and at the
   most aft CG.

   14.10.4.3 After completion of check flights, the pilot will sign the aircraft’s airframe
   maintenance log book, certifying that the aircraft is safe to operate within the
   operational limits of the aircraft’s design. Refer to 14 CFR § 91.407(b).
